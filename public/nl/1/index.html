Quick context if you're new: I started Well Run because every startup list I found was really a funding list. Whoever raised the most sat at the top. I wanted to know who's actually moving. Who's hiring, who's growing, who's quietly losing steam. So I score 700 private companies every week and rank them.

This was the first full week of the chart, and the story wasn't the one I expected.

The winners barely raised

The biggest jump of the week was Column, from #342 to #98. Column is a bank built by William Hockey, one of Plaid's co-founders, and he funded it himself. For months my scoring basically ignored it, because it gave points for VC rounds and Column didn't have any. That was my mistake, not theirs. Once I started crediting companies that grow on their own revenue, it shot up.

Same thing with PLAUD, the little AI note-taking recorder you've probably seen on someone's desk. Profitable, no VC round, 51 open roles. It went from #272 to #55.

And one close to home: Canals, built here in Miami, was profitable before it raised a single dollar from VCs. It joined the board this week in the Race zone.

Three companies, same lesson. The money is the easy part to see. The business is the part that matters.

The ones that slipped

Runpod fell from #84 to #196. Nothing dramatic happened. Hiring cooled a little (30 open roles down to 26) and that was enough to push it from Race to Tempo.

Viktor and Simile each lost about 80 spots for half a point. That's how tight the middle of the chart is right now. A couple of postings coming down can move you a lot.

The big one

Anthropic filed to go public on Monday. Nasdaq, ticker ANT. I read the filing coverage twice because the numbers don't look real.

$16.3B in revenue in the first half of 2026. The same six months a year earlier: $1.2B. That's 13x.

And it's a business business. Almost 12,000 customers pay it more than $100K a year. About 250 pay more than $10M.

The $80.7B net loss sounds scary, but it's mostly a paper charge tied to convertible debt. The operating loss was $693M, down from $2.1B a year earlier.

For Well Run it means one thing. The day it lists, it leaves the chart, because I only track private companies. Enjoy the #1 spot while it lasts.

Still on top

Anthropic is still #1, for now. Databricks has the most open roles on the board, almost 900. Ramp is #3. It passed $2B in annualized revenue, up from $1.5B in June. That's half a billion added in about four months.

My pick: Harbor

Here's a problem I hadn't thought about until this week. AI agents are starting to pay invoices, sign contracts and answer customers. So when one of them messes up, who pays for it?

Right now, nobody really knows. Your insurance was written for mistakes people make. The model company points at you. You point at the vendor. And the bill just sits there.

Harbor wants to be the answer. It finds every agent a company is running, measures how risky each one is (operational, security, regulatory), and blocks the decisions that break the company's own rules before they go through. Whatever risk is left, Harbor insures, with its own money on the line.

That last part is what got me. Plenty of startups will sell you a dashboard that flags risky AI. Very few are willing to pay out when the AI actually gets it wrong. Putting money behind it forces them to get the measuring right.

The team is four Spanish founders who met at Stanford and left to build this, led by CEO Jesus Santos. They hired former founders, math and programming olympiad medalists, and a few pro athletes. They came out of stealth on October 13 with a $10M seed led by Base10, Abstract and 8VC, with NFX and Innovation Endeavors also in. Their first customers are banks and insurers in the US, Europe and Latin America.

Their bet is that one day every AI agent gets insured the way every car and every building is today. If that's even half right, it's a huge market, and right now almost nobody is in it.

I like it because it only exists because of AI. Nobody needed this two years ago.
